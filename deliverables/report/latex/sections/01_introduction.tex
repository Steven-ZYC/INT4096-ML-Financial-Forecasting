\section{Introduction and Educational Motivation}
\label{sec:intro}

Introductory machine learning often uses problems where better models visibly win,
as digits are classified or houses priced. Such examples can hide two questions
that decide whether a model is useful: \emph{what did it learn}, and
\emph{against what baseline}?

Financial forecasting makes both questions concrete. The data are familiar and
plentiful, and short-horizon prices are close to a random walk
\citep{fama1970efficient}. A sophisticated model can therefore lose to the rule
\emph{tomorrow's price equals today's}, and a learner who can explain why has
understood more than a leaderboard shows.

We compare two markets. Apple (AAPL) trades in exchange sessions. Bitcoin (BTC)
trades continuously, with a different volatility pattern and quote unit. The same
pipeline behaves differently on each, so performance belongs to the model--data
pair. Our audience is advanced secondary-school students, pre-university learners
interested in AI, and university students without a machine-learning major. The
intended outcomes are conceptual (Section~\ref{sec:education}).
