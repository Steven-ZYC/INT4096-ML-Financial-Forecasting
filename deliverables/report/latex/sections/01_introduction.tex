\section{Introduction and Educational Motivation}
\label{sec:intro}

Machine learning is usually taught through problems where better models visibly
win: digits get classified, houses get priced, errors fall as capacity grows.
Such examples hide the two questions that decide whether a model is useful ---
\emph{what did it learn} and \emph{against what baseline}.

Financial forecasting is a good corrective: recognisable, data-rich, and hard in
a way that is easy to demonstrate. Short-horizon price prediction is close to a
random walk \citep{fama1970efficient}, so a sophisticated model can lose to the
rule \emph{tomorrow's price equals today's}. A learner who can explain that understands more than a leaderboard teaches.

We therefore compare two contrasting markets. Apple (AAPL) trades on exchange
sessions; Bitcoin (BTC) trades continuously, with a different volatility regime
and quote unit. The same pipeline behaves differently on each, so performance belongs to the
model--data pair. Our audience is advanced secondary-school students,
pre-university learners interested in AI, and university students without a
machine-learning major; the intended outcomes are conceptual
(Section~\ref{sec:education}).
